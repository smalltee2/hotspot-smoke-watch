What this page shows

Fires seen by NASA satellites, and a two-day PM2.5 forecast adjusted to what monitoring stations actually measure. Updated every hour.

How to use it

  • Tap the target button on the map to see PM2.5 and nearby fires where you are.
  • Move the time bar under the map, or press ▶, to see the coming hours.
  • Choose Thai or US AQI under “Show as” in the layers box.
  • Change the region in Settings, and click the map to add your own places, such as home or a school.

Reading the warnings and charts

  • Warnings: by province (average of every 0.1° point in the province), by town (the value at the town) or by monitoring station (measured now, and the forecast adjusted to that station). Each shows the highest 24-h mean PM2.5 in the next 24 h and in 24–48 h side by side, with the lowest–highest temperature and the rain total of the same periods, in µg/m³ or AQI as chosen. Tap an item for its chart.
  • PM2.5 chart: orange = hourly value; blue = 24-hour average (the basis of the Thai AQI); pale band = range across the area (most of the area lies inside it); dark dashed = smoke from fires in our model; horizontal dashed lines = the 15 / 25 / 37.5 / 75 µg/m³ thresholds; vertical line = now.
  • Temperature and rain chart: red line = 2-m temperature (ECMWF downscaled to 0.025°, plus the self-learning correction once it has earned weight); blue bars = rain in each hour, read on the right-hand scale (mm per hour; darker = observed by JAXA GSMaP, lighter = ECMWF forecast). Tap the chart to read an hour.
  • NASA image: point or drag a box: the page judges clear sky, cloud, smoke or haze, or not sure, from several pieces of evidence at the satellite's overpass hour (about 13:30 local): ECMWF cloud cover in the NWS sky terms (above 7/8 cloud, above 5/8 mostly cloudy, at most 3/8 mostly clear) · satellite aerosol optical depth (retrieved = the satellite found it cloud-free; 1 or more = very hazy, below 0.1 = clear sky, after NASA Earth Observatory) · PM2.5 of the forecast map in the Thai PCD bands (above 37.5 and 75 µg/m³) · the fire-smoke share in our model (half or more) · hotspots in the previous 24 h within the alert radius and how many lie upwind (3 or more) · the image colour (the page's own rough description, weighted lightly). Cloud score 2 or more = cloud, smoke score 2 or more = smoke, neither = clear, conflicting = not sure. A rule-based assessment, not a trained ML model.
  • Day boxes: daily average (midnight to midnight), the day's lowest–highest temperature and rain total. Face = PM2.5 level; weather icon = the day's rain in Thai Meteorological Department amounts (0.1–10 mm light, above 10 moderate, above 35 heavy), or without rain the daytime mean ECMWF cloud cover in the US National Weather Service sky terms.

Colours and what to do

Levels follow Thailand's Pollution Control Department (24-hour average). The advice below is general guidance.

Very good (0–15 µg/m³): outdoor activities as usual.
Good (15.1–25): outdoor activities as usual.
Moderate (25.1–37.5): children, older people, pregnant women and people with heart or lung disease should cut down long, hard outdoor activity.
Starting to affect health (37.6–75): everyone should spend less time outdoors; sensitive groups should stay indoors or wear a dust mask; see a doctor if you cough or feel chest tightness.
Affects health (above 75): everyone should avoid outdoor activity, wear an N95 mask outside, and stay in a room with an air purifier if possible.

Good to know

  • This is a forecast, not an official notice. Real values can differ, especially further ahead.
  • Satellites see fires only about twice a day; fires in between, or under cloud, can be missed.
  • For official readings, see the Pollution Control Department's Air4Thai (air4thai.pcd.go.th).

What the page does

It pulls active-fire detections from NASA FIRMS, groups them into fire clusters, converts each cluster's fire radiative power (FRP) into a PM2.5 emission rate, and releases Gaussian puffs that drift with forecast winds from Open-Meteo. Ground-level smoke concentration is summed over all puffs every hour.

Emission

Q [g s⁻¹] = EF [g kg⁻¹] × 0.368 [kg MJ⁻¹] × FRP [MW]

0.368 kg of dry matter burned per MJ of fire radiative energy is from Wooster et al. (2005). The PM2.5 emission factor is chosen by fuel type from the FINN v2.5 table (Wiedinmyer et al., 2023, Table 4; savanna/grassland 7.17, woody savanna/shrubs 7.1, crops 6.43, tropical forest 9.9, temperate forest 15 g kg⁻¹), which updates FINN v1 (Wiedinmyer et al., 2011) mostly with the averages of Akagi et al. (2011). Each detection gets its fuel type from the MODIS IGBP land-cover map (MCD12Q1, Terra+Aqua, previous year; read from NASA GIBS tiles at ≈ 1 km and decoded exactly through the GIBS colour map), mapped to the FINN v2.5 types as in Wiedinmyer et al. (2023, Table 1): evergreen broadleaf → tropical forest within 23.5° of the equator; deciduous broadleaf → temperate forest; shrublands and woody savannas → woody savanna/shrubs; savannas, grasslands, wetlands, cropland/natural mosaics and barren → savanna/grassland; croplands → crops; water, snow and unclassified pixels are dropped, as in FINN. Urban pixels go to savanna/grassland, because FINN's tree-cover rule needs MODIS VCF, which is not used here. A cluster's emission factor is the FRP-weighted mean of its detections. The fuel type chosen in Settings is used where land cover is missing.

Daily fire cycle. VIIRS sees a fire about twice a day (≈ 13:30 and 01:30 local time), near the afternoon peak of burning, so holding the 13:30 FRP for many hours overstates the day's emission. Detections are grouped by 0.1° cell and local solar day, and each group burns along a Gaussian daily cycle on a night baseline, the form used by Zhang et al. (2020) with Himawari and VIIRS:

FRP(t) = FRPnight + A·exp(−(t − 13:00)² / 2σ²), A = (FRPday − FRPnight) / exp(−(tVIIRS − 13:00)² / 2σ²)

so the curve passes through the observed daytime FRP at the overpass; the overpass factor is floored at e−2 and the daytime pulse is capped at 8 h of the observed FRP, so an off-peak detection (for example a morning MODIS pass in an uploaded file) cannot inflate a day's emission. σ = 2.39 h (April–August) or 1.63 h (September–March), the values Zhang et al. fitted for cropland fires in eastern China; Giglio (2007) found tropical burning peaks between 13:00 and 18:30 local time. A fire's daily energy is then FRPnight·24 h + A·σ√(2π), about 4–6 h of the overpass FRP instead of a fixed burn time. These parameters are not yet fitted for Thailand; geostationary (Himawari) FRP would allow that. The older constant-duration option remains in Settings and groups detections by cell only, as before. Puffs are released every 30 min near the daily peak and every 90 min in the weak tail (every 20 min in constant mode); puffs older than 6 h move in 30-min steps and older than 24 h in 60-min steps, which changed town values by about 0.2 % in tests while making the model three times faster. FINN itself provides daily totals and leaves the daily cycle to the user (Wiedinmyer et al., 2011, 2023).

Transport and spread

Meteorology is ECMWF IFS HRES (9 km), fixed by name through the Open-Meteo API so every run uses the same model. The hourly job samples it every 6 h on two nested grids, 0.25° over northern Thailand (96.5–102.5°E, 14.5–21.5°N) and 1° over the whole domain, and the model uses the finer grid wherever it covers the point (bilinear in space, linear in time). Variables: 100 m wind (transport), 10 m wind, shortwave radiation and cloud cover (stability class), boundary-layer height (vertical mixing limit) and precipitation (wet removal). For plume rise, the free-troposphere stability N² = (g/θ)·∂θ/∂z is taken between 850 and 700 hPa from ECMWF IFS 0.25° (the 9 km feed has no pressure levels).

Puffs move with the 100 m wind. Pasquill–Gifford stability class comes from 10 m wind, solar radiation and cloud cover (Turner 1970 table). Spread uses Briggs (1973) open-country σy, σz, with σy not smaller than 0.5 m s⁻¹ × travel time for long-range drift (Heffter 1965). Rain removes particles at the rate Λ = 8×10⁻⁵·P0.79 s⁻¹ (P in mm h⁻¹), the default particle scavenging coefficient of HYSPLIT, so each puff's mass falls by e−Λ·Δt per step.

Plume rise. The plume top follows Sofiev et al. (2012, Eq. 13, fitted to MISR plume heights for all fires): Hp = 0.24·Habl + 170 m·(FRP/1 MW)0.35·exp(−0.6·N²/2.5×10⁻⁴ s⁻²). Emissions are spread evenly from the ground to Hp, as models using this scheme do. Each puff starts with σz = 0.8·min(Hp, Habl), which gives the same ground concentration as an even 0–Hp layer, and the fraction above the boundary layer, 1 − Habl/Hp, is not brought down to the ground. FRP here is the largest single VIIRS pixel in the group, scaled by the daily cycle; Sofiev et al. fitted MODIS 1 km fire pixels, so heights may be slightly low.

C = M / (2π σy²) · exp(−r² / 2σy²) · Fz Fz = 2 / (√(2π) σz) if σz < 0.8 h, else 1 / h

h is the deepest boundary layer the puff has met, so smoke mixed aloft in the afternoon is not squeezed back to the ground at night.

Warning levels

Bands follow Thailand's PCD PM2.5 scale in force since 1 June 2023 (24-h standard 37.5 µg/m³): 0–15, 15.1–25, 25.1–37.5, 37.6–75, above 75 µg/m³. The page applies them to the fire-added part only. Background from traffic, cooking and long-range haze is not included.

AQI

Thai AQI follows the Pollution Control Department bands in force since 1 June 2023 (PM2.5 24-h mean 0–15, 15.1–25, 25.1–37.5, 37.6–75 and above 75.1 µg/m³ → AQI 0–25, 26–50, 51–100, 101–200 and 201+); above 75.1 µg/m³ the page extends the index at the slope of the 101–200 band. US AQI follows the EPA PM2.5 breakpoints revised in 2024 (0–9.0, 9.1–35.4, 35.5–55.4, 55.5–125.4, 125.5–225.4, 225.5–325.4 µg/m³ → AQI 0–50, 51–100, 101–150, 151–200, 201–300, 301–500; higher values are shown as 500), with concentrations truncated to 0.1 µg/m³ and linear interpolation inside each band. Both indices are defined on the 24-hour mean, so in the AQI and 24-h views the map, the station colours and the location box use the mean of the 24 hourly values ending at the selected hour; a mean needs at least 18 of the 24 hours (75 %). Station AQI uses the monitor's own hourly readings, kept by the pipeline for the last 30 hours. The pipeline publishes the previous 24 hours of the corrected field with every run, so the 24-h mean is complete from the current hour onward.

Limits

  1. Screening tool, not a regulatory model. No chemistry or dry deposition (wet removal and plume rise are included); smoke injected above the boundary layer is dropped rather than re-entrained the next day; terrain only enters through the forecast wind and the map regression.
  2. Satellites see a fire only at overpass; fires that start between passes or burn under cloud are missed.
  3. The daily-cycle width σ and the fuel type dominate totals; both use literature values until they are calibrated against monitors in the burning season. Forecast hours after today have no new fires (no persistence).
  4. Check against ground monitors (PCD Air4Thai, CMU CCDC DustBoy) before acting on a warning.

Hourly forecast pipeline

Every hour a GitHub Actions job pulls PM2.5 from reference monitors on OpenAQ, the CAMS global PM2.5 forecast through Open-Meteo, and VIIRS hotspots from NASA FIRMS. Two corrections are applied to CAMS: a Kalman filter per station for the station forecasts, and regression kriging for the map.

Station data are 24-hour averages

Air4Thai stations, which are almost all the reference monitors in Thailand on OpenAQ, report PM2.5 as a 24-hour running mean, not hourly values. In the January–April 2026 record of 186 stations the mean change from one hour to the next is 0.4 µg/m³ and there is no daily cycle, while CAMS varies by about 13 µg/m³ between midday and night. Since 2 October 2026 the page therefore compares like with like for these stations: the Kalman filter and the kriging use the CAMS 24-hour mean ending at the observation hour, station markers in the 24-h and AQI views show the measured 24-hour mean, and the station 24-hour forecast is a blend, w·(latest observed 24-h mean) + (1−w)·(24-h mean of the corrected CAMS), with w depending on the lead time L. In a hindcast of January–April 2026 (193 stations, 6-hourly issues, 24-h means) the blend gave RMSE 8.2 and 10.7 µg/m³ at 13–24 h and 25–48 h ahead, against 8.7 and 12.1 for persistence alone and 19.7 and 20.3 for raw CAMS; Since 4 Oct 2026 w(L) comes from a replay of the live code over January–April 2024, 2025 and 2026 (all monitors, hourly issues, 24-h means): least-squares weights 0.97 at 1 h, 0.94 at 3 h, 0.89 at 6 h, 0.78 at 12 h, 0.68 at 24 h and 0.5 from 48 h (where the fitted 0.59 did not beat 0.5 out of season), linear in ln L in between. Leave-one-season-out RMSE (µg/m³) against w = 0.5: 2.2 vs 3.7 at 1 h, 4.3 vs 5.3 at 6 h, 6.5 vs 7.0 at 12 h, 9.6 vs 9.8 at 24 h. Since 6 October 2026 (SEA-HAF v1.0) stations in the northern fire belt (17.5–22.5°N, 97–105.5°E) give persistence more weight at long leads: 0.74 at 24 h and 0.65 at 48 h, refitted on the belt's reference monitors; tested leave-one-season-out, this lowered the 48-h error (MSE) in the belt by 12 % and raised the share of belt sites that beat persistence at 48 h from 55 % to 87 %, at the cost of catching fewer onsets at 48 h. Shorter leads and all other stations keep the weights above. Stations from other networks (for example Hanoi) report hourly values and are treated as hourly.

From 3 October 2026 the Thai monitors are read directly from the Pollution Control Department (Air4Thai), whose history service gives true hourly PM2.5 (each value is the mean of the hour ending at its time stamp). These replace the OpenAQ copies of the same monitors (any OpenAQ site within 1 km). For them the Kalman filter compares each hourly value with CAMS at the middle of that hour, the kriging uses the 24-hour mean of the hourly values (≥ 18 of 24), and the 24-hour forecast (blue line) is the running mean of measured hours up to now and corrected CAMS after, blended with persistence of the current 24-hour mean. If Air4Thai cannot be reached when the station list is rebuilt, the OpenAQ copies are used as before. The Bangkok Metropolitan Administration's monitors (district offices and parks) are not in the hourly history service; they are read from Air4Thai's live feed, which gives the 24-hour mean used for the AQI, and are treated like the other 24-hour-mean stations. Monitors outside Thailand still come from OpenAQ. Each day's hourly values are kept in data/history (a4t-YYYYMMDD.csv).

Station forecasts: Kalman filter

Each monitor's log-ratio bias b = ln(obs+1) − ln(CAMS+1) is tracked with a scalar Kalman filter (random-walk state), following the predictor bias correction of Delle Monache et al. (2006). The forecast at the station is (CAMS+1)·eb·w − 1, where w = e−lead/48 h fades the correction with lead time. The noise settings (Q = 0.02, R = 0.15 per hour, in log units) and the 48 h e-folding time are starting values, not fitted ones; they will be tuned from the lead-time verification below.

Map: regression kriging with a CAMS drift

At the latest observation hour, the measured 24-hour mean PM2.5 at reference monitors and at quality-checked low-cost sensors (put on the reference scale by a co-location fit, ref = −3.29 + 0.924·sensor, and kept only if they agreed with their neighbours over the previous 30 days) is regressed on the CAMS 24-hour mean and terrain elevation, in log space (24-hour means because that is what the stations report; see above):

ln(PM+1) = β₀ + β₁·ln(CAMS+1) + β₂·z + β₃·ln(BLH) + β₄·U + ε

z is elevation in km from a 0.05° terrain grid (AWS Terrain Tiles, largely SRTM-derived); BLH (km) and U, the 100 m wind speed (m s⁻¹), come from the ECMWF IFS HRES fields above at the observation hour and, for forecasts, at each valid hour. They let the map respond to shallow mixing and calm nights at a finer scale than CAMS; wind speed is also a predictor in the hourly 1 km PM2.5 model for Thailand of Punpukdee et al. (2024). The residuals ε are interpolated by ordinary kriging with an exponential semivariogram fitted each hour (nearest 16 inputs, correction grid 0.025°). This is the regression–interpolation structure used for the EEA European air-quality maps (Horálek et al., 2025), kriging with a chemistry-transport-model drift as in Real et al. (2022), and regression/universal kriging as compared by Beelen et al. (2009). Elevation is included because it improved PM2.5 mapping over Thailand in Han et al. (2022). For forecast hours the map is

ln(PM+1) = ln(CAMS+1) + w·[β₀ + (β₁−1)·ln(CAMS+1) + β₂·z + β₃·ln(BLH) + β₄·U + ε̂], w = e−lead/48 h

so the correction is full at the analysis hour and the map returns to CAMS for long leads. Far from monitors the kriged residual fades to zero and only the regression part remains. The correction is computed on a 0.025° grid (about 2.8 km); real detail comes from the monitors, the sensors and the terrain, while CAMS itself is about 0.4°. In the 2024–26 hindcast adding the low-cost sensors lowered the leave-one-out error of the map from 10.9 to 10.6 µg/m³ at reference monitors and from 23.5 to 19.3 µg/m³ at low-cost sensors; detail finer than about 10 km could not be verified. To prevent extrapolation, the elevation, boundary-layer and wind predictors are clamped to the range the monitors span, fewer predictors are used when fewer than 10 monitors per coefficient report, and the correction is bounded to a factor of three either way (the 2024–26 hindcast showed that without these guards a monitor at the edge of the predictor range could make the correction explode in hours with few reporting monitors). Because the model works in log space, eŷ − 1 is a median-type estimate; the mean would be about eσ²/2 higher (≈ 5–13 % for σ = 0.3–0.5). The page shows the median, and the leave-one-out and lead-time bias below show whether that matters. Leave-one-out scores refit the regression and the variogram without the left-out monitor.

Declustering. Monitors are unevenly spread: about a third are in Bangkok. So that one city does not set the region-wide regression, each monitor is weighted by 1/(number of monitors in its 0.25° cell), scaled so the weights sum to the number of monitors (cell declustering; Deutsch, 1989; Isaaks and Srivastava, 1989), and the regression and its variance are fitted by weighted least squares, also inside the leave-one-out check. The kriging of the residuals is unchanged, since ordinary kriging already accounts for clustered data through the covariance. In a synthetic test with 60 spread monitors (slope 0.90) and 65 clustered urban ones (slope 0.60), the unweighted fit gave 0.79 and the declustered fit 0.88. The same weights are used in the variogram fit: each pair of monitors counts wi·wj, so the thousands of short-distance pairs inside Bangkok do not set the spatial range and nugget used everywhere. Leave-one-out scores are reported overall, declustered, and by area (Bangkok, upper North, rest of Thailand, outside Thailand), because a Bangkok monitor left out is predicted almost perfectly by neighbours a few km away and would flatter an overall score.

Uncertainty layer. The regression-kriging prediction variance (Hengl et al., 2007) is the kriging variance of the residual plus the variance of the regression part: σ² = σ²OK + s²/n + (x₀−x̄)ᵀ·Cov(βslopes)·(x₀−x̄) (the regression variance in centred form), with σ²OK = Σλᵢγ(xᵢ,x₀) + μ from the ordinary-kriging system and Cov(βslopes) = s²(XcᵀXc)⁻¹ from the regression on centred predictors (an ordinary-least-squares stand-in for the generalised-least-squares term). Where the kriged residual is faded out, σ² rises toward the variogram sill. Because the model is in log space, the 68% range is (PM+1)·e±σ − 1, and the map shows eσ − 1 as a percentage. σ is checked hourly by leave-one-out: about 68% of left-out monitors should fall inside ±1σ. It covers the analysis hour only; forecast error grows with lead time (table below).

Fire-emission assimilation (test, shadow mode)

Each hour the pipeline runs our puff model for the fires of the last two days and estimates one emission factor s per fire group (0.5° cell × local solar day) from the station measurements of the last ~48 h: hourly Air4Thai values (mean of the hour), hourly OpenAQ values, and 24-h means (compared with the model's 24-h mean). The innovation is measured − CAMS with each station's slow bias removed; that slow bias (a Kalman filter with a memory of weeks) is learned only from hours with < 1 µg/m³ of modelled fire smoke, so persistent burning-season smoke is not absorbed into it. Only observations with ≥ 1 µg/m³ of modelled fire smoke are used. The factors are log-normal (s = ex, prior x ~ N(0, ln 2²), i.e. a factor of two at 1σ), found by Gauss–Newton minimisation of J = ½(d−h(x))ᵀR⁻¹(d−h(x)) + ½xᵀB⁻¹x (Rodgers, 2000). The observation error R = κ·[max(3 µg/m³, 0.3·y)² + (0.5·F)²] includes transport-model error growing with the modelled fire smoke F; κ is tuned online from the χ² test (2J/m ≈ 1 when the errors are right). The rescaled fires are run once more as an increment Σ(s−1)·F (the model is linear in emissions), which moves with the forecast wind. The corrections are then fitted in sequence: the station Kalman filters (kfA) and the regression kriging (rkA) are fitted to measured − (CAMS + increment) and applied to CAMS + increment, so the same error is never corrected twice. The increment used in those fits is the one forecast for that hour by an earlier run (1–12 h ahead), not the current posterior, which was fitted to the same observations; so the second-stage corrections see an out-of-sample residual. Hourly (hour-ending) measurements are compared with, and corrected on, the CAMS mean of the same hour; the filters of stations that report 24-h running means use an observation error 24× larger, because consecutive hourly updates overlap in 23 of 24 hours. Since 3 Oct 2026 both the public page and this page forecast with the assimilation chain (this page can switch it off in Settings for comparison). Both are logged every hour and scored on the same rows in the verification table. Twin test (known factors ×2 and ×0.5): recovered as 1.80 and 0.50 where well observed.

Self-learning correction (adaptive ensemble)

Every day the pipeline learns the error that remains after the whole chain (CAMS + fire assimilation + Kalman filter) at the hourly stations, r = ln(obs+1) − ln(F+1), from features known at issue time: lead time, hour of day, day of year, the forecast and CAMS, the fire increment and modelled fire smoke, boundary-layer height, wind, rain, the latest measured value and 24-h mean, the station's Kalman bias, and position. Several experts learn on different windows: day (mean error by lead time and time of day over the last 3 days, shrunk toward zero with n/(n+20)), week (ridge regression, 7 days), month (gradient-boosted trees, 30 days) and season (trees on the current Thai season to date plus the same season of earlier years, from our own forecasts and Air4Thai measurements, kept for two years; seasons of the Thai Meteorological Department: summer 16 Feb–15 May, rainy 16 May–15 Oct, winter 16 Oct–15 Feb; starts once 30 days of that season are stored), plus base (no correction). Each expert starts once its window has enough verified data. Every day each expert is scored on the newest verified day with the model it had before that day (out of sample), and the weights follow those scores (exponentially weighted average with fixed share; Littlestone and Warmuth, 1994; Herbster and Warmuth, 1998): skilful experts gain weight, and the weights move back to the plain forecast when the learned experts stop helping. The combined correction is limited to a factor of two and applied for leads up to 24 h (trained on leads to 48 h; in the 2024–26 hindcast the learned correction helped at 24 h but not at 48 h); The stations' learned corrections are also kriged to a 0.1° grid (≈11 km, the same grid as the monitors' kriged residual) every 3 h out to 24 h (same ordinary-kriging machinery, faded to zero far from monitors) and applied to the whole map, the area trends and “my location”, so the map and the station forecasts stay consistent. In a synthetic test with a regime change on day 21, the experts started on days 2, 8 and 31 as their windows filled, the weights followed the change within one to two days, and the out-of-sample error fell from 0.41 to 0.09 (log units).

Since 6 October 2026 (SEA-HAF v1.0) the experts learn from the hourly reference monitors and from the low-cost sensors that pass the rolling neighbour check (one in eight sensor-days, to keep the training set small). In a replay of the 2025 and 2026 seasons this lowered the 24-h-ahead hourly error at low-cost sensors by 17–28 %, and by 14–26 % at sensors that were left out of training, whereas training on reference monitors alone had made the low-cost forecasts 16–23 % worse; the reference monitors kept their gain.

Fine total PM2.5

The fine layer adds sub-grid structure from the fire-smoke model to the regression-kriging map: Cfine = CRK + α·(Cfire − ⟨Cfire⟩0.5°), where ⟨·⟩0.5° is the mean over a CAMS-sized box, computed at 0.025° (coarser for large regions and 24-h views). The ≈0.5° moving average stays approximately equal to the map below it (exactly, except where the sum is clipped at zero), so fire smoke already in CAMS (GFAS) is not counted twice. This follows the idea of sub-grid dispersion without double counting in uEMEP (Denby et al., 2020); here a box-mean high-pass replaces uEMEP's local-fraction bookkeeping, which CAMS does not provide. α is set in Settings and will be calibrated against monitors in the burning season.
