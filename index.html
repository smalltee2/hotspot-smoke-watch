What the page does

It pulls active-fire detections from NASA FIRMS, groups them into fire clusters, converts each cluster's fire radiative power (FRP) into a PM2.5 emission rate, and releases Gaussian puffs that drift with forecast winds from Open-Meteo. Ground-level smoke concentration is summed over all puffs every hour.

Emission

Q [g s⁻¹] = EF [g kg⁻¹] × 0.368 [kg MJ⁻¹] × FRP [MW]

0.368 kg of dry matter burned per MJ of fire radiative energy is from Wooster et al. (2005). EF for PM2.5 is from Akagi et al. (2011). Each cluster emits at its observed FRP for the chosen burn duration after its last detection, released as a puff every 20 minutes.

Transport and spread

Meteorology is ECMWF IFS HRES (9 km), fixed by name through the Open-Meteo API so every run uses the same model. The hourly job samples it every 6 h on two nested grids, 0.25° over northern Thailand (96.5–102.5°E, 14.5–21.5°N) and 1° over the whole domain, and the model uses the finer grid wherever it covers the point (bilinear in space, linear in time). Variables: 100 m wind (transport), 10 m wind, shortwave radiation and cloud cover (stability class), boundary-layer height (vertical mixing limit) and precipitation (wet removal).

Puffs move with the 100 m wind. Pasquill–Gifford stability class comes from 10 m wind, solar radiation and cloud cover (Turner 1970 table). Spread uses Briggs (1973) open-country σy, σz, with σy not smaller than 0.5 m s⁻¹ × travel time for long-range drift (Heffter 1965). Rain removes particles at the rate Λ = 8×10⁻⁵·P0.79 s⁻¹ (P in mm h⁻¹), the default particle scavenging coefficient of HYSPLIT, so each puff's mass falls by e−Λ·Δt per step.

C = M / (2π σy²) · exp(−r² / 2σy²) · Fz Fz = 2 / (√(2π) σz) if σz < 0.8 h, else 1 / h

h is the deepest boundary layer the puff has met, so smoke mixed aloft in the afternoon is not squeezed back to the ground at night.

Warning levels

Bands follow Thailand's PCD PM2.5 scale in force since 1 June 2023 (24-h standard 37.5 µg/m³): 0–15, 15.1–25, 25.1–37.5, 37.6–75, above 75 µg/m³. The page applies them to the fire-added part only. Background from traffic, cooking and long-range haze is not included.

Limits

  1. Screening tool, not a regulatory model. No plume rise, chemistry, deposition or rain-out; terrain only enters through the forecast wind.
  2. Satellites see a fire only at overpass; fires that start between passes or burn under cloud are missed.
  3. The burn-duration setting dominates totals. Compare runs with 3, 6 and 12 h.
  4. Check against ground monitors (PCD Air4Thai, CMU CCDC DustBoy) before acting on a warning.

Hourly forecast pipeline

Every hour a GitHub Actions job pulls PM2.5 from reference monitors on OpenAQ, the CAMS global PM2.5 forecast through Open-Meteo, and VIIRS hotspots from NASA FIRMS. Two corrections are applied to CAMS: a Kalman filter per station for the station forecasts, and regression kriging for the map.

Station forecasts: Kalman filter

Each monitor's log-ratio bias b = ln(obs+1) − ln(CAMS+1) is tracked with a scalar Kalman filter (random-walk state), following the predictor bias correction of Delle Monache et al. (2006). The forecast at the station is (CAMS+1)·eb·w − 1, where w = e−lead/48 h fades the correction with lead time.

Map: regression kriging with a CAMS drift

At the latest observation hour, measured PM2.5 at reference monitors is regressed on the CAMS value and terrain elevation, in log space:

ln(PM+1) = β₀ + β₁·ln(CAMS+1) + β₂·z + β₃·ln(BLH) + β₄·U + ε

z is elevation in km from a 0.05° terrain grid (AWS Terrain Tiles, largely SRTM-derived); BLH (km) and U, the 100 m wind speed (m s⁻¹), come from the ECMWF IFS HRES fields above at the observation hour and, for forecasts, at each valid hour. They let the map respond to shallow mixing and calm nights at a finer scale than CAMS; wind speed is also a predictor in the hourly 1 km PM2.5 model for Thailand of Punpukdee et al. (2024). The residuals ε are interpolated by ordinary kriging with an exponential semivariogram fitted each hour (nearest 16 monitors, residual grid 0.1°). This is the regression–interpolation structure used for the EEA European air-quality maps (Horálek et al., 2025), kriging with a chemistry-transport-model drift as in Real et al. (2022), and regression/universal kriging as compared by Beelen et al. (2009). Elevation is included because it improved PM2.5 mapping over Thailand in Han et al. (2022). For forecast hours the map is

ln(PM+1) = ln(CAMS+1) + w·[β₀ + (β₁−1)·ln(CAMS+1) + β₂·z + β₃·ln(BLH) + β₄·U + ε̂], w = e−lead/48 h

so the correction is full at the analysis hour and the map returns to CAMS for long leads. Far from monitors the kriged residual fades to zero and only the regression part remains. The map is computed at 0.05°; real detail comes from the monitors and the terrain, while CAMS itself is about 0.4°.

Fine total PM2.5

The fine layer adds sub-grid structure from the fire-smoke model to the regression-kriging map: Cfine = CRK + α·(Cfire − ⟨Cfire⟩0.5°), where ⟨·⟩0.5° is the mean over a CAMS-sized box, computed at 0.025°. The 0.5° average stays equal to the map below it, so fire smoke already in CAMS (GFAS) is not counted twice. This follows the idea of sub-grid dispersion without double counting in uEMEP (Denby et al., 2020); here a box-mean high-pass replaces uEMEP's local-fraction bookkeeping, which CAMS does not provide. α is set in Settings and will be calibrated against monitors in the burning season.
